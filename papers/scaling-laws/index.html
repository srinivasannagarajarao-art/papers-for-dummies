Chinchilla: how big a model should you train?

Hoffmann et al., 2022

A budget allocation problem, not a research result. This is the one paper on this site you can put in a funding request.

Download scaling_laws_from_scratch.py — everything below runs from it. NumPy only.

I'm an AI Engineer. I've sized clusters, argued about instance types, and signed off on training runs — and for years I could not have told you, on paper, whether the money was better spent on a bigger model or on more data. I assumed someone upstairs had done the maths. Then I read this paper and found the maths is a two-term curve fit and an argmin over one variable. Twenty lines. The industry got it wrong for two years anyway.

It's sizing infrastructure against a fixed monthly bill

You've done this. Fixed budget, two things to buy:

budget = 10_000        # per month, and it is not going up
# spend it on bigger instances, or on more of them?
for n_instances in range(1, 100):
    size = budget / n_instances
    print(n_instances, size, throughput(n_instances, size))

Chinchilla is that loop. The budget is FLOPs. The two things you buy are parameters (N) and training tokens (D). And there is one hard constraint tying them together, so picking one picks the other: 

C = 6 * N * D                 # your whole training bill, in FLOPs
for N in candidate_sizes:
    D = C / (6 * N)           # tokens you can still afford at that size
    print(N, D, loss(N, D))   # pick the row with the smallest loss

That is the entire paper. Everything else is measuring loss(N, D) carefully enough that the argmin means something.

Why this overturned Kaplan

Two years earlier, Kaplan et al. ran the same experiment and got a different answer: spend almost everything on model size. Their fitted rule was N growing like C0.73 against D like C0.27 — ten times the compute means roughly five times the model and only two times the data. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) is that advice, executed.

Chinchilla redid it with over 400 runs and got N and D both growing like C0.5. Equal split. Grow them together.

Kaplan wasn't sloppy, it was set up differently, and two differences do most of the work. Kaplan used a single cosine learning-rate schedule length across runs of different lengths, which makes short runs look worse than they are; Chinchilla tuned the schedule to each run's actual token count. Kaplan also counted non-embedding parameters. If you want the argument in full, the Chinchilla paper's discussion section spells it out — I'm summarising, not adjudicating.

The one formula

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ   subject to  C = 6ND

Read the loss as three bills added together:

TermShapeWhat it does
EscalarThe floor. What no model in the fit gets below — call it the irreducible part.
A / NαscalarToo small. The penalty for not having enough parameters to represent the function.
B / DβscalarToo little data. The penalty for not having seen enough text.
6NDscalarThe constraint. Fix C, and choosing N fixes D.

The two penalty terms pull opposite ways under the constraint, so the curve is a bowl and the bowl has a bottom. That bottom is the paper.

E, A, B, α and β are the paper's fitted values, not mine. I did not derive a single one of them. The script hardcodes two published sets and re-solves the optimisation they imply — that part is arithmetic you can check.

Where the 6 comes from

Per parameter, per token: 2 FLOPs forward (a multiply and an add), 4 backward (gradient with respect to the activations, gradient with respect to the weights). 2 + 4 = 6. That's the whole derivation. It ignores attention's own quadratic-in-sequence-length term, which stays small while the context is short relative to model width — true for every model in this paper, less true for long-context models today.

An honest wrinkle: which coefficients

Here is the part I nearly got wrong. Chinchilla used three methods — a training-curve envelope, an IsoFLOP sweep, and the parametric fit above — and reported that all three agreed on roughly twenty tokens per parameter. But if you type the parametric fit's printed coefficients into the optimisation, you don't get twenty. You get about ninety.

That's not me making an arithmetic error; it's a known discrepancy, and a 2024 replication refitted the same data and reported coefficients that do reproduce the headline. My script carries both sets and prints them side by side, because "the published numbers don't quite reproduce the published conclusion" is exactly the kind of thing a page like this should say out loud rather than quietly paper over.

Look at the t/p column under Kaplan. It falls below one. At a big enough budget, Kaplan's rule recommends a model with more parameters than training tokens — parameters you cannot afford to train. That is the failure mode, stated as a number.

Same spend, worse model. The interview question here is "why was GPT-3 undertrained?", and the answer is not "they were careless" — it is "the scaling law they were following put the money in the wrong place, and it took 400 runs to show it."

The paper's proof is one head-to-head. Same lab, same data pipeline, roughly the same training bill, radically different split. 

Gopher       N= 280.0B  D= 300.0B  tok/param=   1.1  C=5.040e+23  loss=1.9996
Chinchilla   N=  70.0B  D=   1.4T  tok/param=  20.0  C=5.880e+23  loss=1.9739

predicted loss gap = +0.0257 nats/token in Chinchilla's favour

Chinchilla is a quarter the size and beat Gopher across the paper's benchmark suite. The loss numbers above are what this fit predicts, not measured benchmark scores — don't quote them as the paper's results. The compute ratio is the load-bearing number: 1.167, near enough the same money.

Why LLaMA ignored all of this

Chinchilla optimises the training bill. You also pay to serve the thing, roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter per generated token, no backward pass, every day, forever. Put that in the constraint and the answer moves: 

C_total = 6*N*D_train + 2*N*D_inference
total budget (train + serve) = 5.76e+23 FLOPs

Read it top to bottom. The more tokens you expect to serve, the smaller the model you should train and the longer you should train it. The training loss column gets worse — that is the point. You are deliberately accepting a worse-trained model because you will pay for it a billion times at inference.

Chinchilla answers "best model I can train". LLaMA answered "best model I can run". LLaMA-7B saw about a trillion tokens — roughly 143 per parameter, seven times past compute-optimal. Not a contradiction of this paper; a different objective. That argument is worked through on the LLaMA page.

Where the fit stops being trustworthy

The paper's runs covered roughly 70M to 16B parameters and 5B to 400B tokens. The formula does not know that. Ask it about a model a million times larger and it answers with four decimal places and total composure. 

         N         D  tok/param  predicted loss
     70.0M      1.4B       20.0          3.6587
     70.0B      1.4T       20.0          1.9739
     10.0T    200.0T       20.0          1.8440

the fit's floor is E = 1.8172: at infinite N and D the formula
says loss -> 1.8172 nats/token and never lower.
loss(1e19 params, 2e20 tokens) - E = 1.97e-04

Two things in that block are nonsense and the formula flags neither. A thousand-parameter model does not have a loss of 101. And E is a fitted constant, not a measured entropy of language — it is wherever the curve happened to flatten inside one box of runs, and treating it as a law of nature is how scaling-law arguments go wrong on slide four. Outside the box you fitted, this is arithmetic, not evidence.

இது ஏன் Kaplan-ஐத் தலைகீழாக்கியது

இரண்டு வருடங்கள் முன்பு, Kaplan et al. இதே சோதனையைச் செய்து வேறு பதிலைப் பெற்றார்கள்: கிட்டத்தட்ட எல்லாவற்றையும் model size-க்கே செலவழி. அவர்களின் fit செய்யப்பட்ட விதி — N வளர்வது C0.73 போல, D வளர்வது C0.27 போல. அதாவது compute பத்து மடங்கு ஆனால், model ஏறக்குறைய ஐந்து மடங்கு, data இரண்டே மடங்கு. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) என்பது அந்த ஆலோசனையை அப்படியே செயல்படுத்தியது.

E, A, B, α மற்றும் β என்பவை கட்டுரையின் fit செய்யப்பட்ட மதிப்புகள், என்னுடையவை அல்ல. இவற்றில் ஒன்றைக்கூட நான் கண்டுபிடிக்கவில்லை. Script இரண்டு வெளியிடப்பட்ட sets-ஐ hardcode செய்து, அவை உணர்த்தும் optimisation-ஐ மீண்டும் தீர்க்கிறது — அந்தப் பகுதி நீங்கள் சரிபார்க்கக்கூடிய எளிய கணக்குதான்.

அந்த 6 எங்கிருந்து வருகிறது

ஒரு parameter-க்கு, ஒரு token-க்கு: forward-ல் 2 FLOPs (ஒரு பெருக்கல், ஒரு கூட்டல்), backward-ல் 4 (activations மீதான gradient, weights மீதான gradient). 2 + 4 = 6. இதுதான் முழு derivation. Attention-ன் sequence length-ல் quadratic ஆன term-ஐ இது கணக்கில் கொள்வதில்லை; model width-ஐ ஒப்பிடும்போது context குறுகியதாக இருக்கும் வரை அது சிறியதாகவே இருக்கும் — இந்தக் கட்டுரையின் எல்லா models-க்கும் அது உண்மை, இன்றைய long-context models-க்கு அவ்வளவு உண்மை இல்லை.

ஒரு நேர்மையான சிக்கல்: எந்த coefficients

இதோ நான் கிட்டத்தட்ட தவறாகப் புரிந்துகொண்ட பகுதி. இது நான் செய்யும் கணக்குப் பிழை அல்ல; இது அறியப்பட்ட ஒரு முரண்பாடு. 2024-ல் வெளியான ஒரு replication அதே data-வை மீண்டும் fit செய்து, தலைப்பு முடிவை உண்மையிலேயே மீட்டுத்தரும் coefficients-ஐ வெளியிட்டது. என் script இரண்டு sets-ஐயும் வைத்திருந்து அவற்றை அருகருகே அச்சிடுகிறது. ஏனென்றால் "வெளியிடப்பட்ட எண்கள், வெளியிடப்பட்ட முடிவை அப்படியே மீட்டுத் தருவதில்லை" என்பது, இது போன்ற ஒரு பக்கம் அமைதியாக மூடி மறைக்காமல் வெளிப்படையாகச் சொல்ல வேண்டிய வகையான விஷயம்.

मैं एक AI Engineer हूँ। मैंने clusters size किए हैं, instance types पर बहस की है, और training runs पर हस्ताक्षर किए हैं — फिर भी सालों तक मैं काग़ज़ पर यह नहीं बता पाता कि पैसा बड़े model पर लगाना बेहतर है या ज़्यादा data पर। मैं मान लेता था कि ऊपर किसी ने यह गणित कर रखा होगा। फिर मैंने यह paper पढ़ा और पाया कि गणित सिर्फ़ दो term वाला एक curve fit है और एक ही variable पर argmin। बीस लाइनें। फिर भी पूरी industry दो साल तक इसे ग़लत करती रही।

ध्यान दें: English technical words (compute, budget, tokens per parameter, loss, coefficients, compute-optimal, inference, extrapolation…) मैंने वैसे ही रखे हैं। क्योंकि code में और बाक़ी papers में आपको वही शब्द दिखेंगे। समझ हिंदी में, शब्दावली English में।

यह एक तय मासिक बिल के हिसाब से infrastructure size करना है

यह आपने किया है। तय budget, और ख़रीदने को दो चीज़ें:

budget = 10_000        # हर महीने, और यह बढ़ने वाला नहीं है
# बड़े instances पर ख़र्च करें, या ज़्यादा instances पर?
for n_instances in range(1, 100):
    size = budget / n_instances
    print(n_instances, size, throughput(n_instances, size))

Chinchilla वही loop है। Budget है FLOPs। ख़रीदने की दो चीज़ें हैं parameters (N) और training tokens (D)। और एक सख़्त constraint दोनों को बाँधता है, इसलिए एक चुनना दूसरे को भी तय कर देता है: 

C = 6 * N * D                 # पूरा training बिल, FLOPs में
for N in candidate_sizes:
    D = C / (6 * N)           # उस size पर जितने tokens ख़रीद सकते हैं
    print(N, D, loss(N, D))   # सबसे कम loss वाली पंक्ति चुनें

पूरा paper बस यही है। बाक़ी सब कुछ loss(N, D) को इतनी सावधानी से मापना है कि argmin का कोई मतलब बने।

इसने Kaplan को क्यों पलट दिया

दो साल पहले Kaplan et al. ने यही प्रयोग किया और अलग जवाब पाया: लगभग सब कुछ model size पर ख़र्च करो। उनका fit किया हुआ नियम था — N बढ़ता है C0.73 की तरह और D बढ़ता है C0.27 की तरह। यानी दस गुना compute का मतलब लगभग पाँच गुना बड़ा model और सिर्फ़ दो गुना data। GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) उसी सलाह पर बना है।

Chinchilla ने इसे 400 से ज़्यादा runs के साथ दोबारा किया और पाया कि N और D दोनों C0.5 की तरह बढ़ने चाहिए। बराबर बँटवारा। दोनों को साथ बढ़ाओ।

Kaplan लापरवाह नहीं थे — setup अलग था, और दो अंतर ही ज़्यादातर काम करते हैं। Kaplan ने अलग-अलग लंबाई के runs पर एक ही cosine learning-rate schedule की लंबाई इस्तेमाल की, जिससे छोटे runs असल से ज़्यादा ख़राब दिखते हैं; Chinchilla ने हर run के असली token count के हिसाब से schedule tune किया। Kaplan ने non-embedding parameters गिने। पूरी बहस चाहिए तो Chinchilla के discussion section में यह विस्तार से है — मैं सारांश दे रहा हूँ, फ़ैसला नहीं सुना रहा।

वह एक सूत्र

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ   subject to  C = 6ND

Loss को तीन बिलों के जोड़ की तरह पढ़िए:

Termरूपयह क्या करता है
Escalarफ़र्श। Fit का कोई model इससे नीचे नहीं जाता — यही न घटने वाला हिस्सा है।
A / Nαscalarबहुत छोटा। Function को दर्शाने लायक़ parameters न होने का दंड।
B / DβscalarData कम। पर्याप्त text न देखने का दंड।
6NDscalarConstraint। C तय करो, और N चुनना D को तय कर देता है।

Constraint के नीचे दोनों दंड-terms उलटी दिशाओं में खींचते हैं, इसलिए curve एक कटोरा है और कटोरे का एक तल है। वही तल यह पूरा paper है।

E, A, B, α और β paper के fit किए हुए मान हैं, मेरे नहीं। इनमें से एक भी मैंने नहीं निकाला। Script दो प्रकाशित sets hardcode करती है और उनसे बनने वाले optimisation को दोबारा हल करती है — वह हिस्सा साधारण गणित है, जिसे आप ख़ुद जाँच सकते हैं।

वह 6 कहाँ से आता है

प्रति parameter, प्रति token: forward में 2 FLOPs (एक गुणा, एक जोड़), backward में 4 (activations के सापेक्ष gradient, weights के सापेक्ष gradient)। 2 + 4 = 6। यही पूरा derivation है। इसमें attention का अपना sequence-length में quadratic term नहीं गिना जाता, जो तब तक छोटा रहता है जब तक context, model width के मुक़ाबले छोटा हो — इस paper के हर model के लिए यह सच था, आज के long-context models के लिए उतना नहीं।

एक ईमानदार पेच: कौन से coefficients

यह वह हिस्सा है जिसे मैं लगभग ग़लत समझ बैठा था। Chinchilla ने तीन तरीक़े इस्तेमाल किए — training-curve envelope, IsoFLOP sweep, और ऊपर वाला parametric fit — और बताया कि तीनों लगभग बीस tokens per parameter पर सहमत हैं। लेकिन अगर आप उस parametric fit के छपे हुए coefficients optimisation में डालें, तो बीस नहीं आता। लगभग नब्बे आता है।

यह मेरी गणना की ग़लती नहीं है; यह एक जानी-मानी विसंगति है, और 2024 के एक replication ने उसी data को दोबारा fit करके ऐसे coefficients बताए जो सचमुच उस मुख्य नतीजे को दोहराते हैं। मेरी script दोनों sets रखती है और उन्हें अग़ल-बग़ल छापती है, क्योंकि "छपे हुए आँकड़े छपे हुए निष्कर्ष को ठीक-ठीक दोहराते नहीं हैं" — यह ठीक वैसी बात है जिसे इस तरह के पन्ने को चुपचाप ढकने के बजाय खुलकर कहना चाहिए।

The code

Pure NumPy. No torch, no training, nothing to wait for — the paper is a curve fit and an argmin, and both are a few lines. Every function under 15 lines.

import numpy as np

def compute_flops(N, D):
    """Training FLOPs for N parameters over D tokens."""
    # 2 FLOPs forward + 4 backward, per parameter per token. That is the
    # whole derivation of the 6. It ignores attention's own quadratic term,
    # which is small while context is short relative to model width.
    return 6.0 * N * D


def tokens_for_budget(N, C):
    """Invert C = 6ND: how many tokens does budget C buy at size N?"""
    return C / (6.0 * N)


# Two coefficient sets, BOTH published, NEITHER derived here.
#   PAPER -- Hoffmann et al. 2022, the "Approach 3" parametric fit as printed.
#   REFIT -- Besiroglu et al. 2024 replication, which refitted the same data
#            because the printed coefficients do not reproduce the paper's
#            own headline. Demo 2 shows the gap. REFIT is the default.
#
#   L(N, D) = E + A/N^alpha + B/D^beta
#     E            an irreducible floor: no model in the fit beats it
#     A/N^alpha    the penalty for being too small
#     B/D^beta     the penalty for not having seen enough text
PAPER = dict(E=1.69,   A=406.4,  B=410.7,   alpha=0.34,   beta=0.28)
REFIT = dict(E=1.8172, A=482.01, B=2085.43, alpha=0.3478, beta=0.3658)


def loss(N, D, coef=REFIT):
    """Predicted loss in nats per token for N params trained on D tokens."""
    return (coef["E"] + coef["A"] / N ** coef["alpha"]
            + coef["B"] / D ** coef["beta"])


def optimal_split(C, coef=REFIT, n_lo=1e6, n_hi=1e15, steps=20000):
    """Return (N*, D*, loss*) minimising loss under budget C = 6ND."""
    # THE WHOLE PAPER: minimise L(N, D) subject to 6ND = C.
    # One budget, one free variable: pick N, and D falls out of the
    # constraint. So sweep N and take the argmin. No calculus needed.
    Ns = np.logspace(np.log10(n_lo), np.log10(n_hi), steps)
    Ds = tokens_for_budget(Ns, C)
    Ls = loss(Ns, Ds, coef)
    i = int(np.argmin(Ls))
    return Ns[i], Ds[i], Ls[i]


# The correction the paper deliberately did NOT make. Serving costs about
# 2 FLOPs per parameter per generated token, no backward pass. If you expect
# to serve D_inf tokens, the budget you really control is
#     C_total = 6*N*D_train + 2*N*D_inf
# Same minimisation, different constraint. This is the LLaMA argument.
def optimal_split_with_inference(C_total, D_inf, n_lo=1e6, n_hi=1e15,
                                 steps=20000):
    """Minimise loss subject to 6*N*D_train + 2*N*D_inf = C_total."""
    Ns = np.logspace(np.log10(n_lo), np.log10(n_hi), steps)
    Ds = (C_total - 2.0 * Ns * D_inf) / (6.0 * Ns)   # may go negative
    ok = Ds > 0
    Ls = np.where(ok, loss(Ns, np.where(ok, Ds, 1.0)), np.inf)
    i = int(np.argmin(Ls))
    return Ns[i], Ds[i], Ls[i]


# Kaplan et al. (2020) fitted N_opt ~ C^0.73: spend it on size. Their paper
# gives an exponent, not a constant, so anchor on a model built under that
# advice -- GPT-3. The exponent is theirs; the anchor is mine.
KAPLAN_EXPONENT = 0.73
ANCHOR_N, ANCHOR_D = 175e9, 300e9
ANCHOR_C = compute_flops(ANCHOR_N, ANCHOR_D)


def kaplan_split(C):
    """Kaplan-style (N, D) for budget C, anchored on GPT-3."""
    N = ANCHOR_N * (C / ANCHOR_C) ** KAPLAN_EXPONENT
    return N, tokens_for_budget(N, C)

On the maths anxiety

This paper needs less maths than any other on this site. A power law (A / N**alpha — a thing that shrinks as N grows, quickly at first, then slowly). An argmin (find the smallest row). Multiplication. That's it. There is no gradient anywhere in my script.

The one genuinely mathematical move is curve fitting: they ran 400+ models, wrote down a functional form with five unknowns, and used an optimiser to pick the five numbers that best explain the observations. If you've ever fitted a latency curve to decide an autoscaling threshold, that's the same act at the same level of rigour.

What this paper actually demands is judgement, not algebra: knowing that a fit is only valid inside the range it was fitted on, and that compute-optimal training is not the same objective as cheap serving. Both of those are engineering instincts you already have from capacity planning.

Where to go next

This page gave you the optimisation and the budget arithmetic, and skipped almost everything about how the three estimation approaches were actually run. These fill that in.

  • Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models Hoffmann et al., 2022. The original.

    Read the three approaches and the IsoFLOP plots, which are the visual version of my demo 2 sweep and far more convincing than my table. Then the estimated-optimal table, which is the thing you'd actually cite in a planning doc.

  • Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models Kaplan et al., 2020. The paper this overturned.

    Worth reading second, not first, and read for its method rather than its recommendation. Almost everything it says about power-law behaviour still holds; the conclusion about how to spend a budget is the part Chinchilla replaced.

  • Chinchilla Scaling: A Replication Attempt Besiroglu et al., Epoch AI. Where the refitted coefficients come from.

    The honest wrinkle section on this page, done properly. They extract the paper's own data, refit it, and show why the printed coefficients don't reproduce the printed conclusion. This is what checking someone's arithmetic looks like when it's done in public.

  • Go smol or go home Harm de Vries. The inference-aware version, with the algebra.

    My demo 5 is a sweep; this is the closed-form treatment of the same question — how much extra training compute you accept to get a smaller model, and what that buys you at serving time. The best single thing to read before arguing about model size in a design review.

Order I'd take them in: Chinchilla → de Vries → the replication → Kaplan. Chinchilla gives you the rule, de Vries tells you when to break it, the replication tells you how much precision the rule really has, and Kaplan is history you only need once you care why anyone believed otherwise.
