Generative Adversarial Nets

Goodfellow et al., 2014

Explained as a training loop with two optimisers, because that's what it is — plus the four ways it falls over, which the paper only hints at.

Download gan_from_scratch.py — everything below runs from it. NumPy, plus a tiny CPU PyTorch model for the training step. Twelve seconds on a laptop.

I'm an AI Engineer. I've shipped models, done the MLOps, and I still couldn't read this paper. Not because of the maths — there's one formula and one proof, and both fit on a page. Because nobody told me the trick: the loss function is a second model. Once you see that, the paper is a training loop you've already written, with one extra optimiser. The interesting part is what goes wrong.

The loss function is a model

You know this loop:

for x, y in data:
    loss = criterion(model(x), y)   # criterion is fixed: MSE, cross-entropy
    loss.backward(); opt.step()

The criterion is a constant judge. It never learns. A GAN swaps it for a judge that is learning too — against you:

fake   = G(z)                                                 # forger's attempt
loss_D = -(log(D(real)) + log(1 - D(fake.detach()))).mean()   # detective's turn
loss_D.backward(); opt_D.step()

loss_G = -log(D(G(z))).mean()                                 # forger's turn
loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step()                               # and repeat

That's the whole paper's beating heart. Two networks, two losses, taking turns. The paper's own analogy is counterfeiters versus police; mine is a red team and a blue team, each retraining on the other's latest output. The clever bit is that the forger never sees the real data. All it ever sees is the detective's verdict — the detective is its loss function, and that loss gets sharper every round. Nobody writes down p(x). No likelihood, no loss curve that means "better": just samples and backprop. That is why it worked, and why the logs are so hard to read.

TermMeansProgrammer's analogy
G, generatorNoise in, sample outA procedural generator: seed → level
D, discriminatorSample in, P(real) outA spam filter, retrained every round
zRandom noise fed to GThe RNG seed. Different seed, different sample
pgThe distribution of G(z) over all zEverything your generator can produce, as a histogram
.detach()A tensor with its history cut offA read-only view. Gradients stop here

The one formula

minG maxD V(D, G) = Ex∼pdata[log D(x)] + Ez∼pz[log(1 − D(G(z)))]

Equation 1. Read it as a scoreboard the detective wants high and the forger wants low:

StepShapeWhat it does
z(n, noise_dim)n random seeds. Gaussian noise; nothing learned
G(z)(n, 1)n fakes. Here a point on a line; in the paper, an image
D(x)(n, 1)Probability of "real", between 0 and 1
log D(x)mean over nDetective's score on real data. 0 if it's sure, very negative if wrong
log(1 − D(G(z)))mean over nDetective's score on fakes. 0 if it spots every one
maxD—The detective's turn: push both terms up
minG—The forger's turn: push the second term down. It can't touch the first

In code that is d_loss and g_loss_saturating, two lines each, in the code section below. D maximises the whole thing, so its loss is the negative; G only appears in the second term. Then the one practical trick the paper adds in the paragraph after equation 1: don't train G to minimise log(1 − D(G(z))), train it to maximise log D(G(z)). Same fixed point, "much stronger gradients early in learning". That's g_loss, the non-saturating loss. It is a recommendation, not part of the theory — the proofs are about equation 1. Everyone implements the recommendation. The next two sections show why.

The best detective is a ratio, and −log 4 is the finish line

Section 4 proves two things, and both are arithmetic you can check on a grid with no networks. Proposition 1: for a fixed forger, the best possible detective is D*(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pg(x)). Theorem 1: with that detective the value of the game is −log 4 + 2·JSD(pdata ‖ pg), so its global minimum is −log 4 = −1.386, reached exactly when the forger's distribution equals the data's. My data is three Gaussian bumps at −6, −2 and +2 — the paper's own Figure 1 is a 1-D toy like this. Three candidate forgers, scored in closed form: 

-log 4 = -1.386   <- the value of the game if the forger wins

forger                        D*(-6)  D*(-2)  V(D*,G)   -log4 + 2*JSD
only the bump at -2            1.000   0.250   -0.750     -0.750
all three, twice as wide       0.667   0.667   -1.203     -1.203
p_data exactly                 0.500   0.500   -1.386     -1.386

Where the forger never goes (x = −6, first row) the detective is certain: 1.000. Where the forger matches the data exactly, the detective is a coin flip everywhere, 0.500, and the value bottoms out at −log 4. The last two columns agree to three decimals: that's Theorem 1, checked numerically.

The number you'll be asked about is −log 4. It's log ½ + log ½: a detective that says 0.5 to everything, scored on both terms. It is the forger's win condition, not the detective's — a D loss heading there is good news.

Two losses, and why the trick exists

D is a sigmoid on a logit. Differentiate each forger loss with respect to that logit and each collapses to a single term:

   saturating      log(1 - D)  ->  d/dlogit = -D        -> 0 as D -> 0
   non-saturating  -log D      ->  d/dlogit = -(1 - D)  -> 1 as D -> 0

    D(G(z))    |grad| saturating    |grad| non-sat
      0.500                0.500             0.500
      0.100                0.100             0.900
      0.010                0.010             0.990
      0.001                0.001             0.999

Early in training the forger is terrible and the detective is sure of it: D(G(z)) ≈ 0. The loss as written then has a gradient of ≈ 0 — exactly when the forger most needs one. The trick flips it: the worse you're doing, the bigger the push. Measured on the real networks, after the detective gets a 1000-round head start against a frozen forger: 

   D(G(z)) after the head start: 0.0071
   |gradG| with saturating (eq. 1)         : 0.0213
   |gradG| with non-saturating (the trick) : 2.2216

Same networks, same noise, same detective. A hundredth of the signal. "Saturating" means the sigmoid saturating — the same disease as an unscaled softmax in the attention paper. Numerical hygiene, again.

The game, played properly

One D step, one G step, 4000 rounds, Adam. The script prints the detective's accuracy on real and on fake data, the paper's V, the norm of the forger's gradient, the spread of 1000 fakes, and how many land within 1.0 of each bump: 

   step  D acc real/fake     V    |gradG|  fake std   near -6 / -2 / +2
    500     0.83 / 0.29   -1.363    0.064      3.42      108 /  177 /  308
   1000     0.29 / 0.67   -1.380    0.081      3.10      192 /   95 /  342
   2000     0.64 / 0.58   -1.369    0.116      3.34      210 /  165 /  358
   3000     0.61 / 0.41   -1.377    0.492      3.64      224 /  258 /  346
   4000     0.94 / 0.09   -1.384    0.215      3.78      238 /  272 /  330
  final fakes: mean -2.01  std 3.78  off every bump: 160 of 1000

Three things to read. The last column: fakes on all three bumps, not perfectly even, still evening out. V: parked at −1.38, which is −log 4 to two decimals — the forger has effectively won. And the accuracy pair: 0.94 / 0.09 looks lopsided until you add them. Real plus fake ≈ 1.03 is chance — a detector that says "real" to everything scores exactly 1.00 on that sum. There is nothing left to detect. Note the detective's learning rate is a quarter of the forger's (5e-4 against 2e-3); at equal rates, on this data, the middle bump starves. The game is a balancing act, and the rest of this page is what happens when the balance goes.

The failure modes have names

The theory assumes the detective is optimal at every step and the forger has unlimited capacity. Neither is ever true, and section 6 admits it in a sentence: G must not be trained too much without updating D, "to avoid the Helvetica scenario in which G collapses too many values of z to the same value of x". Everyone who trains one of these hits that and its cousins. Four of them, each one edit away from the healthy run.

Mode collapse: the forger out-runs the detective

Give the forger ten times the learning rate, lr_g=2e-2. Every 100 rounds:

   step  D acc real/fake     V    |gradG|  fake std   near -6 / -2 / +2
    100     1.00 / 0.00   -1.373    0.013      0.27        0 /    0 /    9
    300     0.60 / 1.00   -1.276    0.038      0.31        0 /  992 /    0
    400     0.00 / 1.00   -1.222    0.795      4.68      116 /    1 /    0
    600     0.53 / 0.65   -1.372    0.022      5.74       58 /   52 /  571
    800     0.33 / 1.00   -1.275    0.009      0.24      648 /    0 /    0
   1000     0.54 / 0.46   -1.397    0.042      3.32      276 /   59 /  397

Fake std 0.31: a thousand samples in one heap. At round 300 the heap is on −2, at 600 it's on +2, at 800 it's on −6. The forger finds the one place the detective currently rates "real", moves everything there, the detective catches up, and it hops. It never learns to be in three places at once, because one place is always the cheapest next move. That's the Helvetica scenario, with counts. What did not collapse in my runs: five G steps per D step at the normal rate, and a 1-dimensional z. In 1-D a staircase from one noise value to three bumps is easy to learn; those two bite in 2-D and on images.

The detective wins: the forger's gradient vanishes

Now the opposite. Momentum SGD (what the paper says it used), five D steps per G step (Algorithm 1's k; the paper used 1), and G's loss exactly as equation 1 writes it: 

   step  D acc real/fake     V    |gradG|  fake std   near -6 / -2 / +2
    200     1.00 / 1.00   -0.017    0.044      0.14        0 /    0 /    0
    400     0.99 / 1.00   -0.018    0.073      0.20        0 /    0 /    0
  final fakes: mean +0.18  std 0.20  off every bump: 1000 of 1000

The detective is perfect. V is −0.02 instead of −1.39. And the forger has not moved: 400 rounds in, its samples still sit at 0 with a spread of 0.2, where the random init put them. A perfect detective is a useless teacher. Same run, one change — the non-saturating loss: 

    100     0.67 / 1.00   -0.729    0.671      1.01        0 /    0 /  604
    400     0.67 / 1.00   -0.818    1.334      0.27        0 /    0 / 1000

The forger moves in the first 100 rounds — straight onto one bump, because a strong detective under SGD is still a bad game. But it moves. That's the entire content of the paper's "in practice" paragraph, in two tables.

Why the tutorial you copied doesn't freeze even with the written loss: Adam. It divides each gradient by its running scale, so a gradient of 0.02 and one of 2 produce the same-sized step. On this toy that hides the saturation completely. On images the tiny gradient is also mostly noise, and Adam amplifies noise. Use the trick regardless.

The forgotten detach

In the detective's turn the fakes come out of G. If you don't .detach() them, D's loss backpropagates through D and on into G. The norm of G's gradient right after a D step: 

   detach=True   |gradG| after D's step: 0.0000
   detach=False  |gradG| after D's step: 0.0606
   cosine(leaked gradient, G's own gradient): -1.000

Cosine −1: the leaked gradient points exactly against the forger's own. Of course it does — D's loss wants fakes more detectable. Whether it is ever applied depends on where your zero_grad() sits. Zero G right before its own backward and the leak is wiped; you only paid for a wasted backward pass, which is why this bug is often silent. This script zeroes both networks at the top of the round, so it is applied: 

   step  D acc real/fake     V    |gradG|  fake std   near -6 / -2 / +2
   1000     0.33 / 1.00   -1.081    0.106      1.85      198 /  339 /    0
   3000     0.33 / 1.00   -1.128    0.066      2.21      430 /  430 /    0

A whole bump gone, and the detective at 1.00 on fakes for the entire run. Compare the last column with the healthy table.

Forgetting to alternate: not a game any more

The tempting shortcut: add the two losses, one optimiser over both networks, one backward(). It compiles, it runs, and it is not adversarial. D's own term pushes D(G(z)) down; G's term, now flowing into D's weights too, pushes it up; they cancel where D(G(z)) = 0.5. And G's gradient through the combined loss is zero at exactly that point. Nobody pushes anybody: 

   step  D acc real/fake     V    |gradG|  fake std   near -6 / -2 / +2
    600     1.00 / 0.78   -0.694    0.000      0.00        0 /    0 /    0
   1000     1.00 / 0.77   -0.693    0.001      0.00        0 /    0 /    0
  final fakes: mean +0.03  std 0.00  off every bump: 1000 of 1000

Fake std 0.00 for a thousand rounds: the forger never left its starting point. V is −0.693 = −log 2, which is log 1 + log ½ — the detective says 1 to every real sample and ½ to every fake, and has no reason to say anything else. A game needs two players who each want something the other doesn't. One loss has one wish.

The code

NumPy for the mechanism: the optimal detective and the value of the game, straight from section 4. A tiny PyTorch model, CPU only, for the one part that needs gradients — the training loop. Every function under 20 lines. log is torch.log with a clamp, so log(0) can't happen.

Download the full script — 130 lines of mechanism plus seven runnable demos. Then: 

python3 gan_from_scratch.py
def d_loss(d_real, d_fake):
    """Detective: maximise log D(x) + log(1 - D(G(z)))  ->  minimise minus that."""
    return -(log(d_real) + log(1 - d_fake)).mean()


def g_loss_saturating(d_fake):
    """Forger, as WRITTEN in equation 1: minimise log(1 - D(G(z))).
    When D is confident (D(G(z)) ~ 0) this has almost no gradient. Demo 2."""
    return log(1 - d_fake).mean()


def g_loss(d_fake):
    """Forger, as the paper RECOMMENDS (section 3, the paragraph after eq. 1):
    maximise log D(G(z)) instead. Same fixed point, far bigger gradient early."""
    return -log(d_fake).mean()


def train_step(G, D, opt_G, opt_D, rng, batch, noise_dim,
               k_d=1, k_g=1, g_loss_fn=g_loss, detach=True):
    # Clear both gradient buffers once, at the top of the round.
    opt_D.zero_grad()
    opt_G.zero_grad()

    # (1) THE DETECTIVE'S TURN, k_d times. Real should score 1, fake 0.
    #     fake.detach() cuts the graph: D's loss must not flow back into G.
    for _ in range(k_d):
        real = torch.from_numpy(sample_real(batch, rng))
        fake = G(torch.randn(batch, noise_dim))
        if detach:
            fake = fake.detach()
        loss_d = d_loss(D(real), D(fake))
        loss_d.backward()
        opt_D.step()

    # (2) THE FORGER'S TURN, k_g times. Fresh noise. Push D(G(z)) towards 1.
    #     D's weights get a gradient too, but only opt_G.step() is called,
    #     so D stays put. That is what "taking turns" means in code.
    for _ in range(k_g):
        loss_g = g_loss_fn(D(G(torch.randn(batch, noise_dim))))
        loss_g.backward()
        opt_G.step()

    return -loss_d.item(), grad_norm(G)

Now break it

This is the part that actually makes it stick. Reading won't do it. You debug for a living — reverse-engineering from broken behaviour is your native mode, and with a GAN it's the only mode: there is no loss curve to trust. Then read the paper — but only §3 (one page: equation 1, Figure 1 and the non-saturating trick), Algorithm 1, and the two statements in §4.1, Proposition 1 and Theorem 1. Skip the proofs on the first pass, and skip §5 — the Parzen-window likelihood numbers were the best anyone could do in 2014 and nobody reports them now. Read §6 last: half a page, and it names the failure modes you just produced. You'll find the paper is just the comments you've already read.

On the maths anxiety

Honestly: the maths in this paper is one expectation (an average over samples — a .mean()), one log (so a product of probabilities becomes a sum), and one ratio. Proposition 1 is "maximise a·log y + b·log(1 − y) over y", a one-line calculus exercise with the answer a/(a+b). Theorem 1 is that ratio substituted back in and the result recognised as a known distance between distributions, Jensen–Shannon. Same league as the attention paper.

You don't need to derive the gradients — loss.backward() did — and you don't need §4.2's convergence proof, which assumes a convex space of generators that a neural net violates on line one; the paper says as much right after it. What you do need, and what the maths won't give you, is the engineering: which loss, which optimiser, whose learning rate is bigger, where the detach goes, and how to read a log where the loss going to zero means you lost. That is the interview question, and it's this page.

Where to go next

This page gave you one angle: the GAN as two optimisers taking turns, and the four ways the turns go wrong. Deliberately narrow — no images, no convolutions, none of the fixes.

  • Generative Adversarial Nets Goodfellow, Pouget-Abadie, Mirza, Xu, Warde-Farley, Ozair, Courville, Bengio, 2014. The original.

    §3 and Algorithm 1 are train_step. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 are demo 1. Figure 1 is this page's toy, drawn. Then §6, which admits in advance everything demo 4 showed you.

  • From GAN to WGAN Lilian Weng. Maths, carefully.

    The full derivation of Theorem 1 with KL and Jensen–Shannon written out, then the thing I only named: why JS is a bad distance when the two distributions don't overlap, and how the Wasserstein distance (WGAN) answers it.

  • NIPS 2016 Tutorial: Generative Adversarial Networks Ian Goodfellow. The first author, two years on. Long.

    The taxonomy of generative models I skipped, the failure modes with the theory of why, and a tips-and-tricks section. Skim the tips first; read the rest when you want the map of the field.

  • ganhacks: how to train a GAN Soumith Chintala and others. An engineering checklist, no theory.

    A numbered list of tricks from a DCGAN author: normalise inputs, separate real and fake batches, label smoothing, which loss patterns mean trouble. The runbook for a log that looks like demo 4. Spectral normalisation and the rest of the stabilisation literature came after; this is the pre-flight list.

Order I'd take them in: the paper's §3 → ganhacks → Weng → the tutorial. The page you now can read, then the runbook, then the maths for why the runbook exists, then the survey. If you only read one, read Weng — it's where WGAN, gradient penalties and spectral norm start to make sense as answers to the two failures above.
