Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Christiano et al., 2017 · Stiennon et al., 2020

Explained as an A/B test with a model of the tester in the loop, because that's the only version I could hold in my head.

Download rlhf_from_scratch.py — everything below runs from it. NumPy, plus a tiny CPU PyTorch reward model.

I'm an AI Engineer. I've shipped models, done the MLOps, and I have fine-tuned on human preference data with a library that hid every moving part behind trainer.train(). I could not have told you what the reward model was trained on, why the KL term is there, or why people say "reward hacking" like a proverb. The maths wasn't the problem. Nobody told me the whole thing is an A/B test, with a model of the tester standing in for the tester.

You can't spec it, but you can A/B test it

You know this feeling, and you know the way out of it:

def loss(answer):
    # "helpful, honest, harmless"
    return ???                  # nobody can write this line

def which_is_better(answer_a, answer_b):
    return ask_a_person()       # one bit: "A" or "B". People are good at this

RLHF is what you do with those bits. Fit a model that predicts the vote: the reward model. Turn your language model's knobs to make that model happy: the policy. And keep the policy on a leash, so it can't wander off into answers the reward model has never seen: the KL penalty. 

r      = fit(votes)                          # reward model: answer -> score
policy = climb(r, start=ref, leash=beta)     # optimise r, but stay near ref

That's the whole idea. Two lines, and the second is where the trouble lives.

TermMeansProgrammer's analogy
Reward model r(x, y)A learned score for answer y to prompt x, fitted to votesA model of the A/B tester
Bradley-TerryP(A beats B) = σ(rA − rB)Elo. Two scores, one sigmoid, a probability
Policy πThe model being tuned. A distribution over answersThe thing you ship
Reference πrefThe supervised model you started from. FrozenThe main branch
KL penalty, βHow far π has drifted from πref, charged against the reward; β is the coefficientThe size of diff you're allowed. β is the leash length, tuned by hand

Why votes and not scores? For the reason you run A/B tests instead of surveys: people are inconsistent at absolute scores ("is this a 7?") and consistent at relative ones ("this one"). The noise doesn't vanish, it moves to the close calls, where it does the least damage. Supervised fine-tuning can only imitate; it can't learn that one good answer beats another good answer. A vote can. The three-stage pipeline that wraps all this (demonstrations, comparisons, RL) is the InstructGPT page; read it second.

The one formula

lossRM = −log σ( r(x, ywin) − r(x, ylose) )
objective(π) = Ey∼π[ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π ‖ πref )

Two lines because there are two models. Read each as pipeline stages:

StepShapeWhat it does
r(win) − r(lose)scalarThe margin. Positive means the model agrees with the person
σ(·)(0, 1)Margin → the probability the model gives to the vote that actually happened
−logscalarCross-entropy on one bit. Small when confident and right, huge when confident and wrong
Ey∼π[r]scalarAverage reward-model score of what the policy writes. This is what the optimiser climbs
β · KL(π ‖ πref)scalar ≥ 0Ey∼π[log π(y) − log πref(y)]: how far the policy has drifted, in nats. β = 0 means no leash

Ask which, not how much

The script builds a toy world: 4 prompts, 8 candidate answers each, and an answer is four numbers (correct, length, hedges, polite). A hidden true_reward says what people want: being right is most of it, and past a certain length nobody reads it. The reward model never sees that function, and never sees correct either: it reads text, so it gets sounds, its own guess at correctness, wrong one time in four. The reference policy (the supervised model) writes short-to-medium answers, like its demonstrations did. Prompt 0:

Now ask a person: pick two answers the reference would actually write, show both, record the winner. The person is noisy the Bradley-Terry way, P(A wins) = σ(trueA − trueB), so a close call is a coin flip.

The label is one bit. No 7/10, no rubric, and nothing in the data says why one won. The reward model has to guess the why from what it can see.

The reward model is a model of the tester

A 4→16→1 network, trained on 2000 of those bits with nothing but −log σ(r(win) − r(lose)). The loss goes from 0.6805 to 0.4591 and stops there, because the votes themselves are noisy. The only check that matters is whether its ordering matches the truth, and it does: rank correlation 0.877 over all 32 answers, from 2000 clicks. The scale is its own, because Bradley-Terry only sees differences, so I normalise the way Stiennon et al. do (reference answers score 0 on average; I also scale to unit spread so β means the same for every model here). Only the order is comparable.

Climb it, on a leash

The policy is a softmax over the 8 answers, one row of logits per prompt, and it starts as the reference. Each step: sample answers, score each with the reward model, subtract the leash, nudge the logits. The reward per sample is exactly InstructGPT's, rm(answer) − β · (log π(answer) − log π_ref(answer)): the KL penalty folded into the reward. The papers use PPO. I use REINFORCE, the same objective with the plainest estimator there is: the gradient of log-softmax with respect to the logits is onehot(answer) − probs, so the policy gradient is one line. The optimiser is Adam, as in the papers. rm is what the optimiser sees; true is what you never get to see.

The KL term is not decoration. Same reward model, no leash: true reward peaks at 3.103 and ends at 2.704. Even a decent reward model has soft spots, and an optimiser will find them. The leash is what stops the policy exploiting the model of the tester instead of pleasing the tester.

The reward model is fine where it has data. Off its data it is guessing: the reference almost never wrote a wall of text, so the model saw one twice in 300 answers, and its score out there is whatever a 16-unit network happens to extrapolate. The policy went looking for the highest score, and the highest score is where the guess landed high. That's the bug you will hit. Not "the reward model is wrong", which is always true, but "the optimiser is a search for where it is wrong". At scale, Gao et al. drew this exact curve (arXiv 2210.10760): reward model score up, real preference up then down, as a function of KL.

The leash length

Same thin reward model, three values of β, 300 steps each.

  beta    final rm   final true   final KL
   0.0       1.941        1.758      3.730
   0.5       1.237        2.360      1.143
   2.0       0.385        1.605      0.091

  the reference itself:          true      1.117   KL    0.000

β = 0 wins on rm and loses on true. β = 2 barely leaves the reference. The middle is the point: improve on the reference without leaving the region where the reward model was trained. β is tuned, not derived.

The interview number is not β, it's the KL. "How far has the policy drifted from the model you trusted?" is the health metric of an RLHF run, reported in nats. Asked why the KL term exists, point at the table above this one.

The code

NumPy for the mechanism: the world, the votes, the policy and its gradient. A 4→16→1 PyTorch model for the one part that needs backprop, fitting the reward model. CPU, about four seconds. No function over 33 lines. Below, the two functions that are the paper; the toy world, the votes, the reward-model fit and seven lines of Adam are in the script.

Download the full script — 444 lines including six runnable demos. Then: 

python3 rlhf_from_scratch.py
def bradley_terry_loss(r_win, r_lose):
    return -F.logsigmoid(r_win - r_lose).mean()


def train_policy(rm_table, R_true, ref_logp, beta, steps=300, lr=0.05,
                 batch=64, log_every=25, verbose=False, moving_reference=False,
                 plain_sgd=False, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    logits = ref_logp.copy()                         # start AT the reference
    penalty_ref = ref_logp
    state = [np.zeros_like(logits), np.zeros_like(logits)]
    history = []
    for step in range(0, steps + 1):
        logp = log_softmax(logits)
        probs = np.exp(logp)
        if moving_reference:
            penalty_ref = logp.copy()          # the break: leash tied to self
        if step % log_every == 0:
            rm_now = (probs * rm_table).sum(-1).mean()        # optimiser's view
            true_now = (probs * R_true).sum(-1).mean()        # what you never see
            row = (step, rm_now, true_now, kl_to_reference(logp, ref_logp))
            history.append(row)
            if verbose:
                print("  step {:4d}   rm {:6.3f}   true {:6.3f}   KL {:6.3f}"
                      .format(*row))
        grad = np.zeros_like(logits)
        for p in range(N_PROMPTS):
            a = rng.choice(N_RESP, size=batch, p=probs[p])    # sample answers
            R = rm_table[p, a] - beta * (logp[p, a] - penalty_ref[p, a])
            adv = R - R.mean()                                        # baseline
            grad[p] = (adv[:, None] * (np.eye(N_RESP)[a] - probs[p])).mean(0)
        if plain_sgd:
            logits += lr * grad                                       # the break
        else:
            logits += adam_step(grad, state, lr, step + 1)            # ascent
    return np.exp(log_softmax(logits)), history

Now break it

This is the part that actually makes it stick. You debug for a living — reverse-engineering from broken behaviour is your native mode. Demo 6 runs most of these and prints them side by side.

On the maths anxiety

Honestly: a sigmoid, a log, and a weighted average. The log is there so a product of probabilities becomes a sum. The "expectation" in the objective is a weighted average over what the policy would say, and the KL is a weighted average of log-ratios. That is the whole formula sheet.

The one derivative worth knowing by heart: the gradient of log-softmax with respect to the logits is onehot − probs. It is one line of train_policy and it is the entire policy gradient. PPO's clipping is engineering on top of it; loss.backward() handles the reward model. What separates AI engineers here is not the maths. It is knowing that the reward model is a model, that models are wrong off their data, and that an optimiser is a search for exactly the places where it is wrong.

Where to go next

This page gave you one thing: the reward model as a model of the tester, and the leash as the only thing between you and Goodhart. These fill in what I skipped. The second paper, Stiennon et al., 2020, is the same recipe applied to a language model: the reward model becomes a language model with one output unit, the policy becomes GPT, and the KL penalty is written the way I wrote it. Its over-optimisation figure is my demo 4, measured with real people.

  • Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg, Amodei, 2017. The original.

    Read §2.2 with the script open: it is the code above, with an Atari agent where I have a softmax over eight answers. They also choose which pairs to ask about, a part I skipped entirely.

  • Illustrating Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback Lambert, Castricato, von Werra, Havrilla. Diagrams.

    The three-stage pipeline as pictures, and PPO named properly. Where I said "the papers use PPO, this is REINFORCE", this shows what the swap involves. Best first read if diagrams land better than a printed table.

  • Reward Hacking in Reinforcement Learning Lilian Weng. A survey, with the maths.

    My demo 4 is one instance. This is the taxonomy: why it happens, what has been tried against it, and what it looks like in deployed language models. Go here when "it found the blind spot" stops being enough.

Order I'd take them in: Hugging Face → Christiano §2.2 → Stiennon → Weng. Increasing depth, and each assumes the previous. One more, for later: DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) showed you can skip the explicit reward model and train the policy on the comparisons directly. Read it once you can explain why the leash is in the objective above, because the leash is the part DPO keeps.
