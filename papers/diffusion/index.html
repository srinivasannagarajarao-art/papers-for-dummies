Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020 — and where Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) fits

Explained as a for loop you learn to run backwards, because the variational bound in the paper hid that from me for a year.

Download diffusion_from_scratch.py — everything below runs from it. NumPy for the mechanism, plus a tiny CPU PyTorch model for the training step.

I'd run Stable Diffusion on my own GPU, swapped samplers by name, argued about guidance scales. Then I opened the paper all of that descends from and stalled at equation 3, a variational bound with three kinds of KL term in it. Nobody told me the bound is scaffolding. The method is: corrupt data with a for loop, learn a one-step undo, run the loop backwards.

Learn to undo a for loop

You know this — a loop that ruins something a little at a time:

x = x0
for t in range(1, T + 1):                          # T = 1000 in the paper
    x = sqrt(1 - beta[t]) * x + sqrt(beta[t]) * randn()   # shrink, add noise
# x is static now. No trace of x0 left.

Two facts turn that into a generative model. First, you never have to run the loop: Gaussians compose, so a one-liner jumps to any step t. Second, the only thing that gets learned is a one-step undo: 

x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps   # jump to step t

eps_hat = model(x_t, t)          # the ONE learned thing: "what noise was added?"

x = randn()                      # start from static
for t in range(T, 0, -1):        # the same loop, backwards
    x = undo(x, t, model(x, t))  # subtract predicted noise, add a little back

That's the paper. A corruption function with random access to any stage, and a model that is ctrl-z for one stage.

The one formula

Lsimple = Et, x0, ε ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱt x0 + √(1−ᾱt) ε, t ) ‖2

Read it inside-out, as pipeline stages:

StepShapeWhat it does
t ~ Uniform{1..T}one int per samplePick a random depth into the loop
ε ~ N(0, I)(n, d)Draw the noise you are about to add. This is also the answer key
√ᾱt x0 + √(1−ᾱt) ε(n, d)The jump. xt in one line, no loop
εθ(xt, t)(n, d)The model's guess at ε
‖ε − εθ‖2scalarPlain MSE. Adam does the rest

d is 2 in my script, 32×32×3 in the paper's CIFAR-10 runs. It's a regression: it trains like a classifier where a GAN trains like a fight. The variational bound in §3 shows this MSE is a weighted version of it; §3.4 then drops the weights because the samples got better without them. That is the paper's key practical finding. Equation 14.

The schedule: three arrays, nothing learned

βt goes linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02 over T = 1000 steps (§4 of the paper). αt = 1 − βt. ᾱt is the running product of the alphas: how much of the original signal is still standing at step t. Precomputed once, indexed by t.

From the running code:

  t =  500   alpha_bar = 0.078587   sqrt(alpha_bar) = 0.2803
  t = 1000   alpha_bar = 0.000040   sqrt(alpha_bar) = 0.0064

x_T built by the closed-form jump, 20000 draws from ONE x_0 each:
  x_0            mean of x_T        std of x_T
  [1. 0.]        [ 0.015 -0.009]    [1.002 1.   ]

That second table is the contract. Whatever x0 you start from, xT is N(0, 1), so sampling can start from N(0, 1) without ever seeing the data. Break it (make_schedule(5), in the table below) and sampling starts in the wrong place.

The shortcut: any step in one line

Add two independent Gaussians and the variances add. Chain t shrink-and-noise steps and they collapse to one Gaussian centred on √ᾱt x0 with variance 1 − ᾱt. Equation 4. That is why training never runs the loop: pick a t, jump.

Don't take my word for it. The loop and the jump, side by side:

Training: predict the noise, not the image

Algorithm 1 in the paper is four lines, and my train loop is those four lines with the paper's line number as a comment on each. Draw x0, t and ε, jump to xt, ask the model for ε, MSE. The model is a 3-layer MLP here and a U-Net in the paper. Same job. 

baseline -- always predict zero noise -- scores 1.0000. Beat that.

  step   500   loss 0.2552
  step  1000   loss 0.2279
  step  3000   loss 0.2000
  step  4000   loss 0.1924

  trained 4000 steps on CPU in 4.6s
  held-out loss: 0.1918  ✓

The model gets t as an input, through the same sine/cosine embedding the transformer uses for position (the attention page). It needs it: the same xt could be a ring point plus a little noise or mostly noise, and how much to remove depends on which. Take t away: 

  held-out loss, all t:         with t 0.1918   without t 0.2358
  held-out loss, t <= 50 only:  with t 0.8107   without t 1.1272  ← worse than zero
The question you'll be asked: what does the model predict? The noise, not the image. The paper's ablation table compares predicting the posterior mean against predicting ε; ε wins under the simple loss. Later work predicts x0 too, but the sampler must be told which.

Sampling: the loop, backwards

Algorithm 2. Start from N(0, I). For t = T down to 1: ask the model for ε̂, subtract βt / √(1−ᾱt) of it, divide by √αt, then add fresh noise with variance βt — none on the last step. Watch the batch tighten from a blob onto the ring: 

the batch, mid-flight:
    t = 1000   mean radius 1.250   radius spread 0.659
    t =  500   mean radius 1.230   radius spread 0.636
    t =  100   mean radius 1.004   radius spread 0.318
    t =    1   mean radius 0.991   radius spread 0.123

  real data (ring)                 mean radius 1.001   radius spread 0.100
  generated, 1000 DDPM steps       mean radius 0.991   radius spread 0.123  ✓

The model never sees a ring at sampling time; it only learned "what noise was added", and undoing that a thousand times makes the ring fall out. The "add fresh noise back" step looks like a bug and isn't. Drop it: 

  real data (ring)                 mean radius 1.001   radius spread 0.100
  DDPM, with noise (correct)       mean radius 0.979   radius spread 0.129
  DDPM, noise term removed         mean radius 0.705   radius spread 0.031
  DDIM, 50 steps, eta = 0          mean radius 0.967   radius spread 0.135  ✓

Without the noise the samples shrink toward the centre and lose their spread. The last row is the fix for the thousand-call problem: DDIM (Song et al., 2020) is also deterministic but with corrected coefficients — guess x0 from ε̂, re-noise that guess to a much earlier step. Same model, 50 calls. Every fast sampler in a Stable Diffusion dropdown descends from it.

Where Stable Diffusion fits

Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) is this exact algorithm with two changes and one consequence.

Run it in latent space. First train a lightly regularised VAE that squeezes a 512×512×3 image into a 64×64×4 latent, 48 times fewer numbers. Run Algorithms 1 and 2 on the latents, not the pixels, and decode once at the end.

Condition on text. A text encoder (CLIP's, in the released model) turns the prompt into token vectors. The U-Net gets cross-attention layers: query from the image features, keys and values from the text tokens. The dictionary lookup again, with the image asking the prompt what it should contain.

The consequence is why it runs on a gaming card: the loop runs over 16k latent values instead of 786k pixels. The guidance-scale slider (classifier-free guidance) is a third paper; the loop is unchanged.

The code

NumPy for the mechanism: schedule, forward loop, jump and reverse step are closed-form and need no gradients. A tiny PyTorch MLP, CPU only, trains in about five seconds for the one part that does.

Download the full script — 220 lines plus five runnable demos and six deliberate breaks. Then: 

python3 diffusion_from_scratch.py
def make_schedule(T=1000, beta_start=1e-4, beta_end=0.02):
    betas = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.linspace(beta_start, beta_end, T)])
    alphas = 1.0 - betas
    alpha_bar = np.cumprod(alphas)          # alpha_bar[0] = 1: no noise yet
    return betas, alphas, alpha_bar

def q_step(x_prev, t, betas):
    noise = RNG.normal(size=x_prev.shape)
    return np.sqrt(1 - betas[t]) * x_prev + np.sqrt(betas[t]) * noise

#   x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) * x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) * eps,  eps ~ N(0, I)
def q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bar, eps):
    ab = alpha_bar[t]                                  # scalar, or (n,) per-sample
    ab = ab.reshape(-1, 1) if np.ndim(ab) else ab
    return np.sqrt(ab) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps

def train(model, data, alpha_bar, steps=4000, batch=256, lr=1e-3,
          target="eps", log_every=500):
    T = len(alpha_bar) - 1
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    running = 0.0
    for step in range(1, steps + 1):
        x0 = data[RNG.integers(0, len(data), batch)]       # 2: x_0 ~ q(x_0)
        t = RNG.integers(1, T + 1, batch)                   # 3: t ~ Uniform{1..T}
        eps = RNG.normal(size=x0.shape)                     # 4: eps ~ N(0, I)
        xt = q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bar, eps)                # the jump, no loop
        y = eps if target == "eps" else x0                  # x0 = break-it target
        pred = model(torch.tensor(xt, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(t))
        loss = ((pred - torch.tensor(y, dtype=torch.float32)) ** 2).mean()  # 5
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        running += loss.item()
        if step % log_every == 0:
            print(f"  step {step:5d}   loss {running / log_every:.4f}")
            running = 0.0
    return model

def p_step(x_t, t, eps_hat, betas, alphas, alpha_bar, add_noise=True):
    undo = betas[t] / np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps_hat
    mean = (x_t - undo) / np.sqrt(alphas[t])
    if t == 1 or not add_noise:
        return mean
    return mean + np.sqrt(betas[t]) * RNG.normal(size=x_t.shape)

def sample(model, n, sched, add_noise=True, log_at=(), x_init=None):
    betas, alphas, alpha_bar = sched
    T = len(betas) - 1
    x = RNG.normal(size=(n, 2)) if x_init is None else x_init   # 1: x_T ~ N(0, I)
    for t in range(T, 0, -1):                                    # 2: for t = T..1
        eps_hat = predict_noise(model, x, t)                     # 3: ask the model
        x = p_step(x, t, eps_hat, betas, alphas, alpha_bar, add_noise)   # 4
        if t in log_at:
            m, s = ring_stats(x)
            print(f"    t = {t:4d}   mean radius {m:.3f}   radius spread {s:.3f}")
    return x

Then read the paper — but only §2 for the forward process (equation 4 is the jump), §3.2 for where predicting ε comes from, and §3.4 for Algorithms 1 and 2 and the simple loss. Skip §3.3, the progressive coding experiments and the appendix. The paper is just the comments you've read.

On the maths anxiety

The one fact this paper runs on: add two independent Gaussians and the variances add. That is the entire closed-form jump, and why the square roots are there. Past that it's a prefix product, an MSE and a for loop.

What you don't need: the variational bound in §2 and §3 (three pages of KL terms whose punchline is "use MSE"), score matching, Langevin dynamics, the SDE view that came a year later. All real; none needed to ship a diffusion model or explain one in an interview.

What separates engineers on diffusion work is elsewhere: sampler and step count against a latency budget, guidance scale against prompt fidelity, VAE artefacts, and knowing the noise schedule is a hyperparameter you may change.

Where to go next

This page gave you one thing: diffusion as a loop you learn to reverse, in code you can break. Two other people cover what I skipped.

  • Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020. The original.

    §2 and §3.4 first: equation 4, Algorithms 1 and 2, equation 14. Then the ablation table in §4, the evidence behind "predict ε with the simple loss".

  • What are Diffusion Models? Lilian Weng. The maths, complete.

    Everything I called scaffolding, derived line by line: the posterior q(xt−1 | xt, x0), the bound, how the simple loss falls out of it. Go here when "trust me, the variances add" isn't enough.

  • The Illustrated Stable Diffusion Jay Alammar. Diagrams.

    My Stable Diffusion section as pictures: text encoder, latent grid, U-Net with cross-attention inside it, decoder at the end, drawn as boxes with tensors flowing between them.

Order I'd take them in: the paper's §3.4 → Alammar → Weng. Algorithms first, because you already have them in code; the maths last.
